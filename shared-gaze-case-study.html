Shared Gaze.
Seeing where your partner is looking.

Headsets hide the eyes, and eye contact is how people coordinate. Three studies on how to show a collaborator's gaze in augmented reality without taking away control, privacy or the social side of working together.

VIEW THE PROJECT ↗ Read the paper ↗
ROLE
Lead researcher: system design, study design, development, analysis
YEAR
2025 (PhD dissertation, University of Florida)
TYPE
Three controlled user studies, 72 participants
STACK
HoloLens 2, Unity, MRTK2, C#, Photon, Azure Spatial Anchors
01 / SUMMARY

Giving collaborators back the glance across the table.

Head-mounted displays hide the eyes, and eye contact is one of the quickest ways people coordinate. This research asks whether shared-gaze visualizations (SGVs), which show a partner where you are looking, can replace what the headset removes, in the noisy, task-focused settings of industrial work.

I built a two-headset augmented reality system on stock HoloLens 2 devices and ran three collocated studies with 72 participants in total. Each study fed the next: the first found that overlays and timers hurt control, the second looked at whether you should see your own gaze, and the third added a new outline visualization and tested it against background noise.

02 / THE PROBLEM

Industry 4.0 still needs people, and people need to see each other.

Smart factories automate more each year, but human judgment, adaptability and oversight remain essential. AR lets workers share 3D information, yet two problems appear on the shop floor:

  • Headsets block social cues. The visor hides the eyes, so the glance that says "this one" disappears.
  • Noise degrades speech. Factories are loud, and verbal coordination suffers first.
  • Gaze displays can backfire. Prior work showed visualizations can overwhelm, distract, and take control and privacy away from the person being watched. Little of it had been tested in realistic tasks or for social effects.

Overall question: how can we aid collaborators in industrial tasks through shared-gaze information in augmented reality?

03 / RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Three questions, one per study.

RQ1

Control and privacy

How do we present gaze while preserving users' sense of control and privacy during an assembly task?

RQ2

Self-gaze

How does seeing your own gaze change how you perceive shared-gaze visualizations?

RQ3

Audio noise

How does background noise change those perceptions, and how people use eye contact?

04 / THE SYSTEM

A prototype that runs on stock headsets.

Two HoloLens 2 headsets run a Unity app (MRTK2, C#). A Photon network keeps both worlds in sync and spatial anchors align them to the same physical table. A control laptop sends wizard-of-oz commands and records data. The headsets' own eye trackers drive everything, with a nominal accuracy of 1.5°.

  • Logging. Gaze events are written on change: timestamp, user, object, gaze origin and direction.
  • Eye contact. An invisible 0.2 m sphere around each head detects eye contact, glances at the partner and shared focus, counting both instances and time.
  • Gaze ray. A 1 m red ray, under an inch wide, from each person's eyes along their gaze.
05 / THE VISUALIZATIONS

Four ways to show where someone is looking.

Ray
A constant line from the partner's eyes to what they look at. Immediate, and its origin makes ownership obvious. Also the most intrusive.
Hover
The looked-at object is highlighted with a colour change. Less intrusive, but the highlight can hide the object's own colour.
Trigger
A visualization that appears after the gaze rests on an object for 1.7 seconds. Tested in study 1 only.
Outline
A thin border around the looked-at object, inspired by Photoshop's Magic Wand. Designed in response to the first two studies, so the object stays fully visible.
06 / STUDY 1

Control and privacy.

11 participants built virtual models (a house, a catapult, an aqueduct) in pairs, comparing ray, hover and trigger in a within-subjects Latin square.

What happened

There was no universal favourite. Some people liked the ray's immediacy, others preferred hover. The trigger, which revealed gaze after a 1.7 second timer, reduced people's sense of control, and the system needed to communicate its current state more clearly.

What I changed

  • Offer customizable visualizations rather than one default.
  • Avoid automatic, time-based triggers, and always show the current state.
  • Prefer hybrid hover-plus-object-recognition triggers.
  • Use non-occlusive borders instead of overlays, which became the outline in study 3.
07 / STUDY 2

Should you see your own gaze?

21 participants sorted virtual recycling debris flowing over a real table into physical bins, while spotting highlighted dangerous items. The design was 2×2: ray or hover, with the partner's gaze only (uni-directional) or both people's (bi-directional).

OBJECTS SORTED
Ray > hoverF(1,11)=5.77, p=.035. Directionality had no significant effect.
SURVEY RELIABILITY
α = 0.94Collaboration experience items Q1–Q11
DANGEROUS OBJECTS
No differenceAcross all four conditions

Most people preferred seeing only their partner's gaze, since it was less cluttered. Yet without their own gaze some felt left out and less sure of what they were doing. With hover in the bi-directional condition, ownership became confusing: two highlights on nearby objects made it hard to tell whose was whose. The ray did not have that problem because its origin shows who is looking.

Participants also combined gaze with speech and gesture, so a gaze cue works best as one channel among several.

Design response: let people switch self-gaze on and off, and add explicit ownership cues when more than one gaze is shown.

08 / STUDY 3

Outline, and noise.

40 participants in 20 pairs did the same recycling task. Sound was a between-subjects factor (white noise at a factory-like level, or silence) and the visualization (ray, hover, outline) varied within subjects.

Outline > hoverFriedman χ²=6.65, p=.036, post-hoc p<.05
ENGAGEMENT (Q11)
p = .010Sound × visualization. With sound on, outline beat hover.

Noise did change how visualizations were perceived, and the outline came out best: rated less distracting and more engaging, and it ranked above hover. Performance did not differ across conditions.

The lower eye contact with the outline should not be read as a social loss. It suggests the outline said enough about intent that partners did not need to look at each other to check.

09 / DESIGN PRINCIPLES

What the three studies add up to.

01

Do not occlude what people are looking at

Highlight edges, not surfaces. Overlays hid detail in study 1, and the outline won in study 3.

02

Let people control it

Customizable visualizations, no hidden timers, and self-gaze that can be turned on when needed.

03

Make ownership clear

Use a uni-directional view, or colour, shape or origin cues when several gazes are shown.

04

Design for the environment

Adapt to noise: more explicit cues when speech is hard to hear, subtler ones when it is easy.

05

Support social communication, do not replace it

Measure eye contact as well as task speed, and avoid designs that make people abandon natural behaviour.

06

Be careful with multi-modal controls

Voice commands can collide with conversation, and gestures can restrict movement.

10 / LIMITATIONS

What these studies cannot say.

  • Participants were adult volunteers doing a staged task on a table, not workers on a factory floor.
  • Sessions were short, so long-term use and fatigue are untested.
  • Eye contact was measured with head colliders, which approximate mutual gaze. The HoloLens 2 could not record point-of-view video while the app ran, so I could not review what people saw.
  • Study 1 had 11 participants and no clear winner. Study 3's hypotheses H1–H4 were not supported, and the support for the outline comes from specific subjective measures.
  • The hardware's 1.5° eye-tracking accuracy limits how small an object gaze can single out.
11 / NEXT STEPS

Where this goes.

  • Evaluate adaptive visualizations that change style with noise level or detected communication need.
  • Explore multi-modal control of gaze visibility (voice, gesture) without interfering with speech.
  • Develop scalable ownership cues for teams larger than two.
  • Take the outline into on-site pilots with industrial workers.

Full results and methods are in the published papers and my 2025 University of Florida dissertation.
